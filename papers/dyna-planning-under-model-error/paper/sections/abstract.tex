Model-based agents trade sample efficiency against the risk that the learned model is wrong. We study this trade-off in the smallest setting where it can be measured: tabular Dyna-Q with a last-outcome model on 6x9 mazes whose walls change mid-training (blocking, shortcut) or whose transitions are stochastic (slip 0.3), comparing reimplemented Q-learning, Dyna-Q, Dyna-Q+ and prioritized sweeping over 1--100 planning steps per real step, with 20 seeds for the main comparison and 10 for the sweeps. Planning clearly helps when the model is right (Dyna-Q beats Q-learning on the static maze, paired $p<10^{-5}$), and Dyna-Q+ recovers from a stale model and finds a shortcut where plain Dyna-Q does not, with its advantage growing with the number of planning steps. Under slip 0.3 the benefit of planning disappears: Dyna-Q's mean cumulative reward falls monotonically with $n$ (paired $p=0.0016$, $n=1$ versus 100), and at $n\ge50$ it is no longer distinguishable from Q-learning (means 45.8 and 39.6 versus 55.4, paired $p=0.44$ and $0.14$, 10 seeds). We did not observe the predicted decline of Dyna-Q's post-change reward with $n$ on the blocking maze (the curve is noisy and low at small $n$), and the Dyna-Q+ bonus is fragile: bonus weights $\kappa\ge 0.01$ collapse performance, and the bonus without the untried-action initialisation hurts on the static and stochastic mazes (paired $p<0.001$ against Dyna-Q+ and against Dyna-Q), with only non-significant trends elsewhere. Prioritized sweeping was statistically indistinguishable from Dyna-Q in cumulative and post-change reward at $n=10$, though it earned more in the first 500 steps (paired $p=0.014$). All claims concern a small CPU study with fixed, untuned hyperparameters.
