Agents trained together by self-play (SP) in a cooperative game tend to settle on an arbitrary convention, such as ``always pull lever 3'', that is optimal for the pair but meaningless to anyone else. Two SP agents that were trained independently then pick different conventions and score close to zero when paired: the zero-shot coordination (ZSC) problem~\cite{hu2020other}. The problem matters well beyond toy games; in the Hanabi benchmark~\cite{bard2019hanabi} and in human--AI collaboration~\cite{carroll2019utility,strouse2021collaborating} the partner is not a copy of oneself.

Two families of remedies are prominent. \emph{Other-play} (OP)~\cite{hu2020other} exploits known symmetries of the game: during training the partner plays a randomly relabelled copy of the shared policy, so that arbitrary symmetry-breaking conventions are never rewarded. \emph{Population-based} approaches such as fictitious co-play~\cite{strouse2021collaborating} and its maximum-entropy variant~\cite{zhao2021maximum} train a best-response agent against a set of independently trained partners. Both were introduced and evaluated with deep networks in settings such as Hanabi and Overcooked, where exact evaluation of payoffs is impossible.

We ask a narrower question that a CPU can answer exactly: in three small symmetric games (a lever game, a safe-option matrix game, and a signalling game), with tabular softmax policies and \emph{exactly computed} payoffs, how much do independently trained SP agents lose in cross-play, and what do OP and a reimplemented population method recover? We test four pre-stated hypotheses (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) and report where each fails.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) An exactly evaluated reproduction of the SP self-play/cross-play gap on three games; (ii) a like-for-like comparison of reimplemented SP, OP and a population (FCP-style) method with identical optimiser and step budget; (iii) ablations on the symmetry group, population size, initialisation scale and training length that show that OP in the lever game, with tabular policy gradients at our budget, finds the distinct lever for only about a third of agents, and that in the signalling game OP reaches its cross-play by abandoning communication. All results are small-scale (20 agents $\times$ 5 seeds, tabular policies) and should not be read as a statement about deep-RL ZSC.
