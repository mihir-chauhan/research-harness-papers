We study how vocabulary size $V$ and message length $L$ affect accuracy on held-out attribute combinations and topographic similarity in a Lewis reconstruction game over $4\times4$ attribute-value objects, using small GRU speakers and listeners trained with straight-through Gumbel-softmax or REINFORCE. This is a small CPU study (three seeds, 6000 training steps, reimplemented baselines). Under this budget the main finding is optimisation-limited rather than capacity-limited: channels at or slightly above the 8 bits needed to name the 256 objects are mostly not learned, and held-out accuracy is near zero for both estimators; the larger channel (V16\_L8, 32 bits) is the only main task where learned protocols generalise to some held-out combinations (about 0.4--0.5 held-out accuracy; in the sweeps, V16\_L5 also reaches 0.487), the opposite of the hypothesis that a channel larger than needed hurts generalisation. Training curves show that this is not simple under-training for Gumbel-softmax: at V4\_L4 its train accuracy peaks at 0.24--0.29 within 1400 steps and then collapses to near zero (still near zero at 20000 steps), whereas REINFORCE is still improving at 6000 steps. Learned protocols have topographic similarity between about 0.1 and 0.4, far below an oracle compositional code (1.0), and a fixed random code of equal capacity memorises the training objects at V8\_L4 and V16\_L8 (0.53 train accuracy at V4\_L4, owing to message collisions) but generalises to none. Gumbel-softmax and REINFORCE are indistinguishable on held-out accuracy with three seeds. The study does not establish how a well-trained protocol depends on capacity: Gumbel-softmax is unstable at tight channels with the untuned $\tau=1$ and learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$ used here.
