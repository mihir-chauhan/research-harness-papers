\textbf{Random vs adversarial direction.} Replacing the ascent direction by a random direction of the same radius removes the benefit entirely: on digits at 20\% and 40\% noise the accuracy is \rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} and \rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/digits_n0.4/test_acc/mean:3}, against \rhval{main/sgd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} and \rhval{main/sgd/digits_n0.4/test_acc/mean:3} for SGD, and nearly all flipped labels are memorised (\rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/digits_n0.2/memorised/mean:3} and \rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/digits_n0.4/memorised/mean:3}). With the same radius and the same extra compute, the gradient-aligned direction is therefore what produces the effect here, rather than weight noise of the same Euclidean norm or the extra step. This is a weak control: a random direction in the roughly 26k-dimensional parameter space changes the loss far less than the gradient direction (for SGD at 20\% noise, a step of norm 0.5 raises the training loss by \rhval{main/sgd/digits_n0.2/sharp_rand/mean:sci2} in a random direction against \rhval{main/sgd/digits_n0.2/sharp_adv/mean:4} along the gradient, Table~\ref{tab:main}), so it does not rule out that larger random noise would help. On two-spirals and noise-free digits the random variant matches SGD too (\rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/spirals_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3}, \rhval{abl_sam/sam-random-direction/digits_n0.0/test_acc/mean:3}). This isolates the perturbation direction, not the reason why the adversarial direction helps (e.g.\ clean-example up-weighting versus implicit regularisation \cite{baek2024why}), which we did not test.

\textbf{SAM+WD (failure of naive combination).} Combining the selected $\rho$ and $\lambda$ without joint tuning is harmful on noisy digits: test accuracy \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} $\pm$ \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/std:3} at 20\% noise and \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.4/test_acc/mean:3} at 40\% (chance; at 20\% noise four seeds are near chance, the median is \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/median:3}, and one reaches \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/max:3}, so the mean is bimodal). In the collapsed runs the weights are driven towards zero (weight norm \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.4/weight_norm/mean:2} $\pm$ \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.4/weight_norm/std:2} at 40\% noise against \rhval{main/sam/digits_n0.4/weight_norm/mean:2} for SAM and \rhval{main/sgd+wd/digits_n0.4/weight_norm/mean:2} for SGD+WD). Without noise SAM+WD is comparable to SAM (\rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.0/test_acc/mean:3} $\pm$ \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/digits_n0.0/test_acc/std:3} vs \rhval{main/sam/digits_n0.0/test_acc/mean:3} $\pm$ \rhval{main/sam/digits_n0.0/test_acc/std:3}), and on spirals it is \rhval{abl_sam/sam+wd/spirals_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} against \rhval{main/sam/spirals_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} for SAM, within one seed standard deviation. On noisy digits each regulariser was selected alone at the grid value just below its own collapse (Tables~\ref{tab:rho},\ref{tab:wd}), so together they may over-regularise; we did not run a joint grid, so we cannot say whether a jointly tuned SAM+WD would beat SAM or SGD+WD.

\textbf{Sensitivity.} Both regularisers show a cliff just above their best value on noisy digits ($\rho$: \rhval{main/sam/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} to \rhval{sweep_rho/sam@rho=1.0/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} from 0.5 to 1.0 at 20\% noise; $\lambda$: \rhval{main/sgd+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} to \rhval{sweep_wd/sgd+wd@wd=0.05/digits_n0.2/test_acc/mean:3} from 0.01 to 0.05). SAM's best value gains only \rhval{cmp/main/sgd+wd/digits_n0.2/test_acc/delta:4} over SGD+WD's at 20\% noise. The practical reading is that at this scale a badly chosen $\rho$ (or $\lambda$) can be much worse than plain SGD, and that the comparison with a tuned weight decay is the relevant one.
